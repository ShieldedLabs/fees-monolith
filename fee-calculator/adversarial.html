What could break this

Every dynamic fee mechanism makes claims about behavior. Without falsifiable tests, those claims are aesthetic. Phase 2 is where claims meet scenarios that could kill them. This page lists the scenarios under simulation, the conditions that would invalidate the design, and the gates the mechanism must pass before consensus enforcement.

The falsifiers

Conditions that would invalidate specific design claims at runtime. If any of these triggers on real data, that claim must be downgraded or the design changed.

Priority-tier falsifier

Higher tiers don't deliver monotone inclusion gains under load.

Stability falsifier

Bucket-switch frequency remains high outside shock windows.

Learning falsifier

Observed bidding behavior is better explained by unstable myopic responses than by no-regret learning dynamics.

Privacy falsifier

  • Discontinuous bucket flips can create timing signals.
  • Fast-lane / binary segmentation leaks urgency or wealth and undermines low-entropy goals.
  • Divergent wallet implementations become a fingerprint.

The full audit defines five falsifiers, F1–F5. Two were promoted to gate-level acceptance criteria: F1 (anti-spam) is implicitly covered by E2's shock-recovery requirement plus the floor logic in Layer 3; F5 (manipulation cost) became gate E4 directly. The four falsifiers above are the runtime-behavioral conditions.

The scenarios

Six adversarial probes target specific failure modes. Each card lists the gate(s) the scenario stresses, the falsifier(s) it could trigger, and what failure would look like.

Burst spam

An attacker floods blocks with high-fee transactions for a short window, pushing the lookback median up. Tests whether the estimator returns to baseline once the burst ends and whether the cost to mount the burst is prohibitive.

Tests
E2 E4 E5
Could trigger
Stability falsifier — if recovery is slow.
Failure mode
Estimator stays elevated for many blocks after the burst ends, or attack cost is small.

Median poisoning

Sustained elevated-fee transactions over the lookback window, designed to shift the median upward without triggering congestion detection.

Tests
E4 E5
Could trigger
Stability falsifier.
Failure mode
The shifted median persists at the new level after the attack stops, or per-bucket cost is small.

Bucket-boundary nudging

Small fee adjustments near the powers-of-10 quantization threshold, designed to flip the bucket cheaply. A direct attack on the bucketing mechanism itself.

Tests
E4 E5
Could trigger
Stability falsifier · Privacy falsifier (timing signals from bucket flips).
Failure mode
Bucket flips are cheap to induce, creating frequent 10× discontinuities and observable timing patterns.

Fast-lane flap

Synthetic demand displacement designed to oscillate the congestion signal, triggering the Priority tier on and off. Hits the underspecified fast-lane semantics that the audit flagged.

Tests
E3 E5
Could trigger
Priority-tier falsifier · Privacy falsifier (binary segmentation leaks urgency).
Failure mode
Priority tier provides no monotone inclusion gain, or its on/off signal leaks user-side urgency.

Miner self-dealing

A miner includes their own transactions priced to push the median up, then pockets most of the cost back as block reward. If the cheap-to-manipulate radius is large, miners can move the recommended fee for free.

Tests
E4
Could trigger
Stability falsifier.
Failure mode
Manipulation cost net of self-recovery falls below an acceptable threshold.
Literature
Bao 2024 studies the closely related family of undercutting attacks. Two parameters from her thesis — normalized block-size limit and fee-gradient sparsity — become tunable knobs in this scenario.

Sybil split

An attacker splits one transaction into many small ones to exploit oracles weighted by transaction count rather than action count. Also stresses divergent wallet implementations as a fingerprint surface.

Tests
E4 E7
Could trigger
Privacy falsifier (wallet-implementation divergence).
Failure mode
Tx-weighted oracles produce different recommendations than action-weighted ones, becoming a wallet fingerprint.

Controls and comparisons

Three additional scenarios are not adversarial — they isolate assumptions or compare controllers. They are listed separately so they don't blur into the attacks.

Floor-fee saturation

Sustained low-volume transactions priced at the floor, no congestion. Tests the audit's claim that anti-spam resilience is dominated by the fee floor itself — that the floor is a security parameter, not a tuning knob.

AIMD comparison

Compares the comparable-median controller against an Additive-Increase Multiplicative-Decrease variant. Feeds extended research gate E12 (controller benchmark).

Hysteresis comparison

Compares controllers with and without hysteresis on bucket flips. Informs E5 transition stability and feeds E12.

About the simulator

The adversarial scenario suite is being developed by external collaborator Mohak Nahta. Findings from specific runs will be published separately. The framework — the scenarios, controllers, oracles, and the harm-ratio metric — is open source: github.com/mnm458/zcash-dyanmic-fee-sim.

The mechanism design audit is by Nicolás "nikete" Della Penna. The MinerSelfDealing scenario draws on Claire Bao's 2024 MIT thesis on undercutting attacks.
