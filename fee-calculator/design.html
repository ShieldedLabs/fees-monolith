The Design

Why the mechanism is shaped this way

The dynamic fee mechanism for Zcash is an estimator, not a new consensus rule, and not an auction. It reads recent confirmed blocks, computes an action-weighted median, rounds it to a power of ten, and publishes the result over RPC. Wallets and indexers consume it. Below: why each piece is necessary, the alternatives rejected, what is still open, and the forward-looking considerations (multisig, ZIP-218, Crosslink, Tachyon).

Three principles

Three words govern every design choice.

Simple

Easy to implement, easy to explain, easy to deploy. Policy-first, consensus later. No build tooling, no complex state machines.

Private

Fees must not leak information that segments users. Discrete buckets (powers of ten) over continuous bidding. Use only already-public data — confirmed blocks, not mempool.

Market-based

Prices emerge from supply and demand, not admin parameters. The mechanism reflects real congestion and lets users express urgency.

The four-layer stack

Each layer serves a distinct function and rests on the one below it. Layers 1 and 2 are active on mainnet today; Layer 3 is a draft policy proposal; Layer 4 is a draft consensus change.

Layer 4 · draft consensus · ZIP-235
NSM fee burn

The teeth. Removes 60% of transaction fees from circulation permanently and pays 40% to miners. Makes sustained spam permanently costly by destroying the attacker's capital. Tightly coupled with Layer 3: as the fee level rises during congestion, the burn rate amplifies the cost of attack.

Layer 3 · draft policy · this site
Dynamic fee estimation

The signal. The z_getstandardfee RPC publishes a fee recommendation derived from confirmed blocks. Lookback of 50 blocks, buffered by 5. Slack capacity in each block is filled with synthetic floor-fee transactions, so the median responds smoothly when the network is quiet. Result is rounded to the nearest power of ten. Two tiers: Standard (median) and Priority (10× Standard, for guaranteed inclusion when blocks fill).

Policy-only. No consensus change required. Wallets and indexers adopt voluntarily.

Layer 2 · active policy · today's network
BLOCK_UNPAID_ACTION_LIMIT

The gatekeeper. Defines how many "unpaid action units" a block can absorb — transactions paying less than the conventional fee consume this budget. The effective network policy today is 0: underpaying transactions are rejected outright.

The Paris Metro tiers in Layer 3 (Standard / Priority) provide service tiering through voluntary overpayment rather than through adjustments to this limit.

Layer 1 · active consensus · ZIP-317
Action accounting

The foundation. Defines the logical action abstraction that unifies transparent inputs/outputs, Sprout JoinSplits, Sapling spends/outputs, and Orchard actions into one accounting unit. Sets a marginal_fee per action (currently 5,000 zats) and a grace_actions threshold (2 actions free).

conventional_fee = marginal_fee × max(grace_actions, logical_actions)

The algorithm in detail

1
Look back 50 blocks, skipping the 5 most recent for stability. For every non-coinbase transaction, compute fee / max(2, logical_actions).
2
Fill unused capacity with synthetic entries. For each block, the unused bytes (out of 2 MB) are filled with synthetic transactions priced at the floor of 1,000 zats / action. This pulls the median toward the floor when the network is quiet.
3
Take the median, round to the nearest power of 10. Ties round down. The floor of 1,000 zats is enforced as a minimum. The result is standard_fee.
4
Publish the priority tier. The priority_fee is always 10× the standard_fee. A separate congestion flag (set when zero synthetic entries were added — every block in the window was full) tells wallets when paying priority actually buys faster inclusion.

Deliberately rejected

Three alternatives surfaced early and were rejected for specific reasons. We list them to make the trade-offs visible.

Just lower fees

Reducing the fixed marginal fee helps short-term cost but reopens the spam economics that caused the 2022 sandblasting episodes. A static answer to a dynamic problem.

Increase block size

Ineffective when the network is not persistently congested. Introduces separate trade-offs around bandwidth, storage, and validator hardware requirements. Doesn't address pricing at all.

EIP-1559 directly

Good responsiveness, but frequent fee changes leak information about congestion timing, require immediate consensus coupling, and are harder to deploy incrementally. Considered and rejected in favor of a policy-first comparable-based mechanism with discrete buckets.

Open design choices in v0

Exploratory analysis (2026-05-09). An early oracle × floor sweep on Mohak's simulator gives a first signal: at floor=1000 (the v0 candidate), both oracle types produce identical harm_ratio = 0 across five scenarios; at floor=5000 (current ZIP-317 mainnet value), action-weighted-median shows harm_ratio = 0.359 on the miner-self-dealing scenario while median-of-medians stays at 0. One seed — not a verdict, but a first signal that oracle and floor are coupled. More on Further Research; full data: sweep summary · JSONL.

Other open questions

Beyond the v0 parameter choices, these structural questions remain unresolved and would shape any future refinement or consensus hardening.

  • How should BLOCK_UNPAID_ACTION_LIMIT interact with the dynamic fee estimation? If the RPC publishes a Standard quote, should the limit expand or contract to match?
  • Should the limit itself become dynamic — wider during low congestion, tighter during high?
  • How do the Paris Metro tiers map to a richer fee alphabet? Is Standard / Priority sufficient, or does the mechanism need more tiers?
  • Should the mechanism account for ongoing chain data costs — storage fees and cost on the commons?
  • Under what conditions should policy-level rules be promoted to consensus enforcement? Strong, durable policy-level adoption across wallets and miners is necessary but probably not sufficient.
Still being worked out

Future considerations

Questions the estimator surfaces but does not answer. None blocks the rollout; each shapes where the work goes next.

Multisig and short transaction expiry

One wallet feature is a short transaction expiry: let a stuck transaction expire quickly and resubmit it at the priority rate. That collides with multisig and threshold signing, where the fee, and the note-commitment anchor, can move while signers coordinate. The blunt interim answer: a multisig wallet should use a long expiry and pay the priority fee at the last signing. Long expiry covers a slow ceremony; priority absorbs fee drift. Priority and expiry are independent, so paying priority does nothing for a transaction that has already expired: the long expiry is the part that matters here.

The residual cost is a privacy one: a long ceremony signs against an old anchor, which is valid but identifying. The real fix is deeper than a fee policy. Signers can authorize the weakest sufficient condition, an intent, rather than one exact transaction, and a recursive proof shows the final transaction satisfies it, assembled later with a fresh anchor and the current fee. That same machinery sidesteps the drawbacks of dedicated post-quantum threshold signatures, and it is the kind of thing Tachyon makes practical. Tracked as a direction, not a v0 feature.

ZIP-218 (shorter block times)

ZIP-218 shortens the block interval to roughly 25 seconds. It does not change the estimator's shape, but it rescales two constants: the 5-block reorg buffer should grow (about 3x) to preserve the same wall-clock margin, and synthetic fill, currently sized by bytes, may instead be sized by ZIP-128 action limits. The short-expiry feature also lengthens in block terms: a 2-minute window is about 2 blocks today and roughly 5 under ZIP-218. ZIP-218 has not shipped as of this writing.

What dynamic fees do not decide

Where the money goes is a separate question

The estimator sets the total fee level from observed congestion. How that revenue is allocated across roles, miners today and finalizers under Crosslink, is a separate policy concern. The separation is deliberate: congestion pricing can evolve independently from fee-splitting policy.

Crosslink

Hybrid PoW/PoS finality without replacing Proof-of-Work.

Crosslink adds a Proof-of-Stake finality layer on top of Zcash's existing Proof-of-Work chain. A sidecar daemon runs BFT consensus and produces finality certificates that wallets and explorers can show alongside the usual confirmations. The fee interaction is allocation-only: Crosslink introduces a new role, finalizers, that must be paid to sustain the BFT layer. Dynamic fees set the total level from congestion; a future ZIP defines the split between miners and finalizers. The two designs are orthogonal, and the finalizer share depends on how Crosslink's validator economics land.

NSM: ZIP-235

The Network Sustainability Mechanism. Burns 60% of transaction fees, pays miners 40%.

ZIP-235 removes 60% of transaction fee revenue from circulation permanently and pays 40% to the block's miner. The 60/40 split is calibrated against a security argument: a split more favorable to miners would give them a rollback incentive, since the direct miner share is fully theirs while the burn benefits them only in proportion to future hashrate. The interaction with dynamic fees is amplification: as the estimator raises the level during sustained congestion, the burn scales with it, so inducing congestion becomes permanently expensive. This is the "teeth" of the design. The marginal-fee reduction (5,000 to 1,000) and this burn must stay compatible, so the minimum fee stays divisible by the 60/40 split; see the specs.

Tachyon

Privacy-preserving wallet sync, still in research.

Tachyon is oblivious synchronization infrastructure that scales wallet sync without leaking what a wallet is looking for. It has no ZIP yet. If it ships with relay-style incentives, dynamic fees would need to allocate a share of revenue to sync infrastructure, analogous to the Crosslink finalizer role. It also reframes the estimator at scale: if capacity becomes abundant, the congestion-pricing function mostly goes dormant and the floor becomes the dominant market. And the same recursive-proof machinery is what makes the intent-based multisig fix above practical. Specifics await design work.
