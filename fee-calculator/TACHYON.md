Interactions with Crosslink and Tachyon
Crosslink and Tachyon make the “externalities” framing more concrete by turning what would otherwise be diffuse costs into identifiable services with clear beneficiaries. With Crosslink, finalizers provide an additional security service—timely finality—that reduces reorg risk and shortens the “time-to-safe-settlement” for users, exchanges, and applications. That means a portion of fees can be interpreted not just as payment for inclusion (miners) but as payment for finalization as a distinct service (finalizers). This aligns better with the reality that “security” and “settlement assurance” are resources users consume under congestion, separate from raw block space.
Tachyon focuses on improving the user experience of shielded syncing by enabling oblivious synchronization against untrusted servers. In that model, the cost of wallet scanning, witness management, and continuous chain tracking is partially offloaded to dedicated sync infrastructure. This is a clearer target for compensation than trying to precisely price the marginal “scanning externality” directly: rather than measuring how much harm a transaction imposes on all wallets, the ecosystem can pay for the infrastructure that makes that harm tolerable. In other words, Tachyon suggests a path where fees support a market for privacy-preserving sync services, which users can opt into without forcing consensus-critical measurement of “wallet unhappiness.”
There is also an intriguing conceptual overlap: the proofs produced by oblivious sync services (e.g., proofs related to witness updating or serving correctness/availability) may be usable as proofs of replication or availability, and thus could inform incentive mechanisms in PoS or PoW contexts. In principle, a protocol could treat “being a well-provisioned, up-to-date sync service” as a utility-bearing contribution and reward it—either directly (explicit payments) or indirectly (as an input into reward allocation). However, elevating such proofs to consensus-critical status risks adding new verification costs, new denial-of-service surfaces, and new centralization pressures; the safest near-term approach is to treat this as future work, while keeping any sync payments market-based and opt-in.
Practically, the dynamic fee mechanism proposed here remains agnostic to the ultimate fee-splitting policy: it aims to set a total fee level that responds to demand and congestion. Crosslink and Tachyon primarily affect how one might justify and allocate that fee revenue across roles—miners for inclusion, finalizers for settlement/finality, and sync infrastructure for user experience—without requiring the proposal to solve the full “price the true marginal externality” problem upfront.

This is a well-structured section. A few observations on where the reasoning is strongest and where it might benefit from tightening:

**The "fee as payment for a distinct service" framing is compelling.** Decomposing the total fee into inclusion (miners), finalization (finalizers via Crosslink), and sync infrastructure (Tachyon) gives each fee component an economic justification tied to a concrete service rather than an abstract externality. That's a much more tractable design space than trying to price diffuse costs like "wallet scanning burden" at the consensus layer.

**The Tachyon paragraph handles a subtle point gracefully** — the move from "measure the harm a transaction imposes on all wallets" to "fund infrastructure that makes the harm tolerable" is a pragmatic reframing that sidesteps what would otherwise be an impossible measurement problem. It's worth emphasizing that this isn't just a simplification but arguably the *correct* abstraction level: users care about sync latency, not about the internal cost accounting of witness updates.

**The paragraph on proof reuse (sync proofs → availability/replication proofs) reads as appropriately cautious**, but you might consider whether it needs to be here at all. It introduces a speculative idea, immediately disclaims it, and concludes with "future work." If this section's purpose is to explain how Crosslink and Tachyon *concretely* interact with the fee proposal, the speculative overlap might distract. On the other hand, if part of the audience will ask "could sync services become consensus participants?", preemptively addressing it is valuable. That's a judgment call about your audience.

**One thing that could be sharper:** the final paragraph says the fee mechanism is "agnostic to the ultimate fee-splitting policy," which is the right design boundary. But it might help to say one sentence about *why* keeping fee-setting separate from fee-allocation is architecturally important — e.g., it allows the allocation policy to evolve (adding new roles, adjusting splits) without requiring changes to the congestion-pricing mechanism itself. That reinforces modularity as a deliberate design choice rather than just a scope limitation.
